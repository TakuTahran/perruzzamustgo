Community safety is one of the issues residents can experience very differently.

For one person, safety may mean being able to walk home from a transit stop after dark.

For another, it may mean preventing youth violence, having safe spaces for children, improving a dangerous crossing, getting help during a mental-health crisis or knowing who to contact when a non-emergency problem occurs.

That is why community safety cannot be reduced to one program or one agency.

In the Jane Finch area, the City of Toronto's Community Development Plan treats safety and wellbeing as a combination of prevention, community support, youth opportunities, mental-health services, safer public spaces and better mobility.

The Jane Finch planning area covers parts of both Ward 7 and Ward 6, so its consultation findings should not be presented as the views of every Ward 7 resident. But the plan provides a useful documented framework for understanding practical safety measures.

## 1. Give Young People More Safe Places and Activities

One of the clearest themes in Toronto's community-safety planning is prevention.

The Jane Finch Community Development Plan calls for opportunities to enhance and expand **supervised recreation and sports facilities for youth**.

It also calls for more culturally responsive programming, skill development and stronger connections between young people and community organizations.

That approach matters because community safety is not only about responding after something happens.

It is also about creating places where young people can spend time safely, build relationships, develop skills and access trusted adults.

Practical measures could include:

* Longer recreation-centre hours
* Sports and arts programming
* Mentorship
* Youth leadership opportunities
* Evening and weekend programming
* Employment preparation
* Accessible programs for youth with disabilities
* Programs designed with local youth rather than only for them

The City's current Jane Finch plan specifically identifies youth-focused community development as part of its safety strategy.

## 2. Continue Violence-Prevention and Intervention Programs

Toronto already operates community-based violence-prevention initiatives.

The **TO Wards Peace** program includes a Northwest site serving Jane and Finch and Rexdale.

Its model works with community organizations and violence-disruption workers who have lived experience and aims to identify escalating conflicts, interrupt violence and connect residents with stabilizing supports.

The program also funds youth-led Peace Projects focused on violence prevention, intervention, healing and community cohesion.

This type of approach is different from simply responding after a violent incident.

It attempts to intervene earlier.

Residents can reasonably ask:

How many young people are being reached?

Which neighbourhoods receive programming?

Are services available throughout the year?

What happens after a short-term grant ends?

How are results measured?

Those questions can help distinguish a temporary initiative from a sustained community-safety strategy.

## 3. Connect Safety With Youth Employment

Employment can also be part of prevention.

Toronto's 2026 Summer Safety Plan includes youth employment, sports, arts, mental-health supports and violence-prevention programs.

The City says its 2026 budget includes **$5 million for youth violence-prevention programs** within SafeTO's broader annual community-safety and wellbeing investment.

In Jane Finch, current City-listed programming has included youth mentorship, employment-readiness opportunities, leadership development and violence-prevention activities.

This raises an important practical question:

**Can young people find meaningful opportunities before they become disconnected from school, employment or community supports?**

A long-term approach to safety can therefore include:

Training

Paid employment

Apprenticeships

Mentorship

Entrepreneurship

Leadership opportunities

And connections to education.

These measures do not guarantee that violence will disappear.

But they address some of the social conditions the City's prevention strategy is designed to target.

## 4. Improve Mental-Health Crisis Response

Not every safety-related situation requires the same response.

Toronto's **Community Crisis Service** provides a non-police-led response for eligible non-emergency mental-health crises.

Residents aged 16 and older can call **211** to connect with the service.

It operates citywide, 24 hours a day, seven days a week, using trained civilian crisis workers.

Services can include:

Crisis intervention

De-escalation

Mental-health support

Referrals

Case management

Connections to housing, food and social services

Post-crisis follow-up.

This provides residents with another response option when an immediate police, fire or ambulance emergency is not occurring.

For emergencies where people or property face immediate danger, residents should still call **911**.

## 5. Make Sure Residents Know Which Number to Call

Community safety can become more confusing when residents do not know which service handles a particular problem.

A simple system helps.

### Call 911

For immediate emergencies requiring police, fire or paramedics.

### Call 211

For non-emergency mental-health crisis support and connections to community and social services.

### Call Toronto Police Non-Emergency

For police matters that are not currently emergencies.

Toronto lists **\*TPS (\*877) from a mobile phone or 416-808-2222** for non-emergency police matters.

Examples can include reporting an incident that is no longer in progress or providing additional information about a previous report.

### Call 311

For many City service issues such as damaged sidewalks, roads, street infrastructure and municipal maintenance concerns.

Clear public information about these different channels can prevent residents from being passed between agencies.

## 6. Improve Lighting in Public Spaces

Sometimes community safety is very physical.

A pathway may feel different during daylight than it does late at night.

Poor lighting can affect residents walking:

From transit stops

Through parks

Along pedestrian paths

Between apartment buildings

To community facilities

The Jane Finch Community Development Plan specifically calls for the City, TTC and other divisions to work together to improve **pedestrian-scale lighting in public spaces**.

That creates a practical accountability question.

Residents can identify:

The exact pathway

The closest intersection

Which lights are missing or inadequate

When the area is most heavily used

Who owns or manages the property

A location-specific report is more actionable than saying an entire neighbourhood is poorly lit.

## 7. Improve Pedestrian Crossings

Road safety is also community safety.

A resident should not have to choose between a long detour and an unsafe crossing to reach school, transit or a community facility.

The Jane Finch Community Development Plan includes actions to improve pedestrian crossings at locations identified through the area's mobility strategy.

Community consultation also raised concerns about pedestrian safety during construction, including the need for safe crossing arrangements.

Potential improvements can include:

Better crossing locations

Longer pedestrian crossing times

Accessible curb ramps

Clear construction detours

Improved lighting

Safer connections to bus and LRT stops

Pedestrian-priority improvements near schools

For each location, residents should be able to ask:

**What has been identified as the problem, what solution is being considered, and when will residents receive an update?**

## 8. Improve Safety Around Transit

Transit safety involves more than what happens inside a bus or train.

Residents also have to reach the stop.

That means looking at:

Lighting

Sidewalks

Crossings

Waiting areas

Visibility

Construction detours

Accessibility

And the walking route home.

Someone getting off a bus late at night may experience a transit trip differently from someone travelling during the afternoon.

Improving the full route between homes, stations, stops and community destinations can therefore be part of a broader safety strategy.

## 9. Support Conflict Resolution and Community-Led Prevention

The Jane Finch Community Development Plan includes another practical measure: strengthening education around **conflict resolution, violence prevention and mental-health awareness**.

It also identifies culturally relevant programming, informal mentorship and support for vulnerable neighbours.

Community organizations may sometimes be able to reach residents who do not initially engage with larger institutions.

That makes partnerships important.

But partnerships should also be accountable.

Residents can ask:

Which organizations are funded?

What services are they providing?

Who can access them?

How long is funding available?

What outcomes are being tracked?

These questions can help residents understand whether a program is actually operating rather than simply being announced.

## 10. Listen to Young People Directly

City consultation in Jane Finch has also emphasized that young people should not simply be discussed as a community problem.

They should participate in designing solutions.

During Jane Finch Initiative engagement, City staff emphasized involving youth throughout the process, supporting community-led initiatives and treating young people as leaders in developing responses.

That can mean asking young residents directly:

Where do you feel unsafe?

When?

Why?

What would make that location safer?

Which programs would you actually use?

What prevents you from accessing existing programs?

Without those questions, governments may invest in services that young people do not use.

## 11. Avoid Stigmatizing Entire Neighbourhoods

Community-safety reporting also carries a responsibility.

One incident should not be used to characterize an entire neighbourhood.

Likewise, the presence of young people, people experiencing homelessness, a shelter, a particular cultural group or residents using a public space is not itself evidence of criminal activity.

Safety reporting should identify:

The specific concern

The location

The date

Available evidence

Who reported it

The agency responsible

And what action was taken.

Jane Finch community engagement has itself raised concerns about stigma and called for it to be actively addressed.

Local reporting should therefore discuss genuine concerns without reinforcing unsupported stereotypes about the community.

## 12. Keep Parks and Community Spaces Active

Public spaces can contribute to safety when people can use them regularly.

The City's Jane Finch planning work describes a vision for parks and public spaces that are connected, welcoming, active and safe.

Community engagement has supported better amenities, programming and activation of parks and public spaces.

Practical measures can include:

Recreation programming

Good lighting

Clear paths

Maintained facilities

Activities for different age groups

Accessible entrances

Regular community use

Safe connections to transit

A public space that is frequently used can serve a very different community function from one residents feel unable to access.

## 13. Measure Whether Safety Programs Are Working

Announcements alone do not tell residents whether a safety initiative is successful.

Programs should have measurable outcomes.

Depending on the initiative, useful information might include:

Number of residents served

Youth participation

Program retention

Hours of programming provided

Employment or training placements

Crisis calls handled

Referrals completed

Community feedback

Locations receiving infrastructure improvements

Dates when promised improvements were completed

Toronto's Community Crisis Service, for example, publishes a dashboard with monthly service metrics.

Similar transparency can make other community-safety initiatives easier to evaluate.

## 14. Residents Need Regular Updates

Safety concerns should not disappear from public discussion after a meeting or announcement.

For a specific neighbourhood concern, residents should be able to find:

**Problem**

What is happening?

**Location**

Where exactly is it happening?

**Responsible organization**

Who can act?

**Action**

What has been proposed or approved?

**Funding**

Has money been committed?

**Timeline**

When should something happen?

**Latest update**

What has changed since the last report?

**Outcome**

What did residents experience afterward?

This framework can be used for youth programs, pedestrian safety, recreation facilities, lighting and other community-safety initiatives.

## What Practical Steps Are Already Identified?

Based on current City plans and programs, documented actions worth following include:

**Expanding supervised youth recreation and sports opportunities**

**Supporting mentorship, conflict-resolution and violence-prevention programming**

**Connecting youth with employment and skill-building opportunities**

**Continuing community-based violence interruption programs**

**Providing non-police mental-health crisis response through 211**

**Improving pedestrian crossings**

**Improving pedestrian-scale lighting**

**Making parks and public spaces safer and more active**

**Improving access to City safety and wellbeing programs**

**Giving residents and young people a role in designing local solutions**

These approaches address different kinds of safety concerns and involve different City departments, agencies and community organizations.

## Community Safety Requires More Than One Response

There is no single program that can address every safety concern in Ward 7.

Emergency response matters.

So does prevention.

Youth programming matters.

So do jobs and mentorship.

Mental-health support matters.

So do safe sidewalks, crossings, lighting and public spaces.

The more useful question is therefore not simply:

**“Is Ward 7 safe?”**

It is:

**“What specific problem are residents experiencing, what practical response is available, who is responsible, and can residents track whether it improves?”**

That provides a clearer way to discuss community safety without reducing a diverse community to statistics, stereotypes or campaign slogans.
