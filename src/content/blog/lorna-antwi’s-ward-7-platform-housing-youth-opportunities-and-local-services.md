# Lorna Antwi’s Ward 7 Platform: Housing, Youth Opportunities, and Local Services



Ward 7 residents have several choices in Toronto's 2026 municipal election, and understanding what each candidate is actually proposing is more useful than relying on campaign slogans alone.

**Lorna Antwi is a registered candidate for Toronto City Council in Ward 7, Humber River–Black Creek.** Public candidate directories list her registration date as May 11, 2026.

Her campaign presents a platform focused on affordable housing and tenant protections, community safety and mental-health supports, youth employment and mentorship, streets and infrastructure, small businesses, food security, families with disabilities, affordability for seniors and safe community spaces.

The important question for residents is:

**What does each proposal mean in practice, what could a city councillor actually influence, and what details still need to be explained?**

## Who Is Lorna Antwi?

Antwi's campaign describes her as a lifelong Humber River–Black Creek resident who attended Brookview Middle School and later Seneca Polytechnic at York.

Her campaign also says she has worked with children, youth, families, seniors, newcomers and refugees through community advocacy and social services, and currently works as a counsellor with Toronto Shelter & Support Services. These biographical details are statements published by her campaign.

She says those experiences have exposed her to issues including housing instability, food insecurity, unemployment, mental-health challenges and barriers facing young people and families.

That professional and community-service background is a central part of how her campaign presents her candidacy.

But experience and policy are separate questions.

Residents can also examine what she proposes to do if elected.

## Affordable Housing and Tenant Protections

Housing is one of the most prominent parts of Antwi's platform.

Her campaign says she would advocate for:

**Rent control**

**Stronger eviction prevention**

**Faster housing construction**

**Reducing waits for subsidized housing**

and

**Support for first-time homebuyers facing affordability barriers.**

Her community-priorities page also highlights rising rent, eviction risk, overcrowding, homelessness and barriers facing first-time buyers.

These are substantial issues, but they involve different levels of government and different policy tools.

## Rent Control Is Not Controlled by One Toronto Councillor

This distinction is important when reading the platform.

Ontario's **Residential Tenancies Act** establishes the provincial rent-increase framework, and the provincial government determines the annual rent-increase guideline.

For 2026, Ontario's guideline is 2.1 per cent for most rent-controlled units; the Province has set the 2027 guideline at 1.9 per cent. Units first occupied for residential purposes after November 15, 2018 are generally exempt from the provincial guideline.

That means a Toronto councillor cannot independently change Ontario's rent-control law.

A councillor could advocate for provincial changes and participate in municipal housing policies, budgets and tenant-support programs.

The distinction matters because residents should know whether a campaign commitment requires:

**City Council action**

**Provincial action**

or both.

## Toronto Does Have Municipal Tenant-Protection Tools

Although the City does not set Ontario's rent-control guideline, Toronto does operate programs aimed at preventing eviction and supporting tenants.

For example, the City's **Eviction Prevention in the Community program**, or EPIC, provides case-management assistance to qualifying low-income households facing imminent eviction.

Toronto also funds the **Toronto Tenant Support Program**, which supports community organizations working on eviction, affordability and tenant issues.

The City also provides renter information, Rent Bank support and guidance on navigating eviction processes, while the formal eviction process itself falls under Ontario's Landlord and Tenant Board framework.

So a useful follow-up question for Antwi would be:

**Which specific municipal tenant-protection program would she expand or change, and what funding would that require?**

That would turn a broad housing commitment into something residents could later track.

## What Does “Faster Housing Construction” Mean?

Antwi's campaign also calls for faster housing construction.

But housing construction involves several different stages.

A councillor may participate in:

Planning and zoning decisions

Budget decisions

City-owned housing initiatives

Development approvals

Infrastructure planning

Affordable-housing agreements

And advocacy with provincial or federal governments.

Residents should therefore ask what part of the process Antwi wants to accelerate.

For example:

Would she support faster planning approvals?

Would she prioritize affordable and non-profit housing?

Would she seek additional City-owned development?

Would she support higher-density development near transit?

How would she address concerns about displacement?

Would proposed new housing include measurable affordability requirements?

Those details are not yet set out extensively on the campaign's public platform.

## Youth Opportunity Is Another Major Platform Theme

Antwi's campaign places significant emphasis on young people.

Her stated platform calls for expanding:

**Youth employment**

**Skills training**

and

**Mentorship opportunities.**

The campaign says the goal is to provide young people with clearer pathways to employment and success.

Her community page says residents have raised concerns about employment, mentorship, training and access to safe recreational spaces.

This focus overlaps with existing City programs.

Toronto already operates youth employment and skills initiatives, including **Building Skills Through Recreation**, a free program for people aged 13 to 24 that provides employment-related skills and certifications connected with recreation, sports, aquatics and community leadership.

The policy question is therefore not simply whether youth programs should exist.

It is:

**What would Antwi add or change?**

## What Could a Ward 7 Youth Plan Look Like?

A more detailed youth policy could identify measurable actions.

For example:

How many additional employment placements should Ward 7 receive?

Would additional City funding be requested?

Would recreation centres extend youth hours?

Would mentorship be delivered through community organizations?

Would the program target teenagers, young adults or both?

Would apprenticeships or skilled-trades pathways be included?

Would employers receive incentives to recruit locally?

How would the City measure whether participants actually moved into employment?

Antwi's current campaign material identifies employment, mentorship and skills training as priorities, but it does not yet provide that level of implementation detail.

That gives residents a clear area for follow-up.

## Streets, Parks and Infrastructure

Everyday municipal services are another major element of Antwi's campaign.

Her platform promises to advocate for:

**Cleaner streets**

**Faster pothole repairs**

**More parks with pools**

and

**Safe gathering spaces for families.**

Her community page similarly says residents are raising concerns about cleaner streets, safer public spaces, infrastructure and pothole repairs.

These are issues much closer to core municipal responsibilities.

Toronto currently allows residents to report potholes through 311, and the City says crews aim to repair reported potholes within four days, depending on conditions.

Road and sidewalk maintenance also includes inspections, preventive maintenance and rehabilitation.

## “Faster Pothole Repairs” Needs a Measurable Target

Because Toronto already publishes a pothole service standard, residents can ask Antwi a particularly specific question:

**What would “faster” mean?**

Would she seek:

A shorter City-wide repair target?

Dedicated Ward 7 maintenance crews?

Additional Transportation Services funding?

More proactive road inspection?

Better public reporting?

More rapid response for high-risk locations?

The City's 311 Service Standard Dashboard already publishes performance information for selected services and allows residents to examine service volumes and performance.

That creates the possibility of measuring whether any future service change actually improves response times.

## Local Services Could Be Easier to Track

Residents already have access to Toronto's 311 system around the clock.

311 handles non-emergency City service information and allows residents to submit and track service requests.

The 311 mobile app also provides tracking numbers, status updates, location tools, photographs and optional notifications.

For a Ward 7 councillor, one potential accountability question would be:

**How would the councillor's office help residents follow unresolved 311 cases?**

For example, an office could publish recurring information about:

Outstanding infrastructure concerns

Repeated service problems

Road and sidewalk issues

Local project timelines

And City-department responses.

Antwi's current platform emphasizes responsive local services, but it does not yet publish a detailed Ward-level service-tracking model.

## Families and Children With Disabilities

Antwi's platform includes a separate commitment to families raising children with disabilities.

Her campaign calls for:

**Accessible community services**

**Inclusive recreation**

**Educational supports**

and additional resources for families.

Her community-priorities page similarly says parents and caregivers have raised concerns about finding accessible services and inclusive recreation.

Some aspects of this area fall within City responsibility, especially recreation, community facilities and certain social services.

A useful candidate follow-up would therefore separate:

**What City Hall can change directly**

from

**What the councillor would need to advocate for through another institution.**

## Seniors, Families and Property Taxes

Antwi's platform also addresses affordability for seniors and families and calls for property-tax fairness.

A separate campaign plank says she would seek lower property taxes through responsible City spending and reductions in waste.

Property taxes are directly connected to City budgeting, so this creates a particularly important follow-up question:

**If tax revenue is reduced or increases are limited, which spending changes would make the budget balance?**

Toronto City Council has to approve a balanced operating budget.

So a detailed tax proposal would ideally specify:

What spending would be reduced?

Which efficiencies would be pursued?

Which services would be protected?

Would capital projects change?

Would new revenue sources be proposed?

The campaign's current public page does not yet provide that budget-level detail.

## Platform Promises and Delivered Policy Are Different Things

Antwi is currently a candidate rather than an incumbent Ward 7 councillor.

That means her campaign platform describes what she says she would pursue if elected; it is not a record of City Council outcomes she has already delivered in the role.

That distinction is important when comparing candidates.

For a challenger, residents can examine:

Experience

Specificity of proposals

Understanding of municipal powers

Funding plans

Measurable targets

And accountability commitments.

For an incumbent, residents can additionally compare previous commitments with Council votes and completed outcomes.

The two kinds of records should not be confused.

Antwi's campaign presents a broad agenda centered on affordability, housing stability, youth opportunity and neighbourhood services.

**Who has authority to approve it?**

**Where would the funding come from?**

**How would progress be published?**

Those questions allow residents to assess the platform for themselves without relying on either campaign promotion or opposition messaging.
