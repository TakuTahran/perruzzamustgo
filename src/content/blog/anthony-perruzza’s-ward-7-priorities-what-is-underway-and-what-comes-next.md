# Anthony Perruzza’s Ward 7 Priorities: What Is Underway, and What Comes Next?



Anthony Perruzza has been part of Toronto municipal politics for a long time.

City records say he served as Toronto City Councillor for the former Ward 8 from **2006 to 2018**, became councillor for the newly configured Ward 7 in 2018 and was re-elected in 2022. His earlier public roles included service as a school trustee, North York councillor and Ontario MPP.

That experience gives Ward 7 residents a substantial public record to examine.

Some major projects associated with priorities promoted by Perruzza's office are now visibly moving forward.

Others remain in planning, have uncertain delivery dates or depend on decisions that one councillor cannot make alone.

There are also examples where Perruzza took a position at Council but did not receive enough support for the outcome he sought.

For residents, the useful question is therefore not simply whether a project has been mentioned by the councillor.

It is:

**What has actually been approved, what is physically underway, what remains unfinished and what should residents expect next?**

## A Clear Positive: The Western North York Community Centre Is Under Construction

One of the strongest examples of visible progress in Ward 7 is the **Western North York Community Recreation & Child Care Centre at 60 Starview Lane**.

Perruzza's office has repeatedly identified the project as one of its community priorities, and he joined Mayor Olivia Chow and City staff at the June 2025 groundbreaking.

This is no longer just a proposal.

The City reports that construction is actively progressing.

By August 2026, work included structural steel, electrical and mechanical rough-ins, concrete work and mass-timber installation for the childcare portion of the facility.

The planned facility will include:

A six-lane 25-metre lap pool

A leisure pool

A childcare centre for up to 62 children

A multi-sport gymnasium

A walking track

Fitness and dance studios

A teaching kitchen

Art and multimedia spaces

A STEM lab

Community meeting spaces.

The City currently lists **Winter 2028** as the anticipated construction completion period, while warning that the schedule can change.

This is an important distinction in assessing an incumbent's record.

The project can reasonably be described as **underway**.

It should not yet be described as a completed facility serving residents.

It is also a City capital project involving Council-approved funding, City staff, contractors and multiple years of planning, rather than something delivered by one councillor alone.

## Another Concrete Action: Ward-Based Maintenance Teams

Perruzza also introduced a 2026 Council motion calling for a pilot program that would create **Ward-Based Maintenance Teams**.

The proposed teams would handle practical neighbourhood work including:

Litter and illegal dumping cleanup

Grass cutting and landscaping

Minor tree trimming

Pothole repair

Minor sidewalk repair

General public-realm maintenance.

City Council adopted the motion in April 2026 and directed the City Manager to report back on a pilot program.

That shows Perruzza successfully moved the idea into the formal City process.

But residents should also know what happened next.

When the City Manager's response returned to Council in July 2026, Council did **not** immediately implement the pilot.

Instead, on a motion from Perruzza, Council referred the matter to the first Infrastructure and Environment Committee meeting of 2027.

So the accurate status is:

**The proposal entered the formal Council process, but a functioning ward-based maintenance team has not yet been delivered.**

That makes it an issue worth tracking in 2027.

## The Jane Finch Community Hub Remains an Important Unfinished File

Perruzza's Ward 7 website continues to list the **Jane and Finch Community Hub and Centre for the Arts** as a project he is working to advance.

The project has a substantial official history.

In 2022, Toronto City Council authorized the City to enter into an agreement with Metrolinx to acquire part of **2050 Finch Avenue West** for the proposed community hub.

That was a real institutional step.

But acquisition and planning are not the same as opening a community centre.

In the current official records reviewed for this article, Toronto Everyday did not find a recent construction-completion date or public opening date for the full Jane Finch Community Hub and Centre for the Arts.

That makes the next questions straightforward:

**Has the full project funding been secured?**

**What design or approval stage is it currently in?**

**When is construction expected to begin?**

**When should residents reasonably expect the facility to open?**

A project that has appeared in public discussion for years should be tracked through dated milestones rather than repeated only as an ongoing priority.

## Firgrove Redevelopment Is Moving — But Major Future Phases Still Have No Construction Date

Perruzza's office also identifies **Firgrove redevelopment** as one of its Ward 7 priorities.

Current City planning records show that significant future work remains.

Toronto's 2026 Housing Development Office work plan identifies **Firgrove Block 2** for continued feasibility work.

The City currently estimates **107 net new rental homes**, all identified as affordable or rent-geared-to-income housing for that block.

But the same City document lists:

**Construction start: TBD**

and

**Anticipated occupancy: TBD.**

The work plan points toward planning approvals and pre-construction activity in 2027, followed by additional pre-construction and financing work in 2028.

That means Firgrove should not be described simply as either a success or failure.

Parts of the revitalization process have moved forward.

But major future housing delivery still has important unanswered questions about timing, financing and construction.

For residents, the next useful milestone is not another announcement.

It is a confirmed construction schedule.

## Weston Road Shows the Limits of a Councillor’s Influence

One of the clearest examples of a Perruzza position not prevailing at Council involves **3406–3434 Weston Road**.

In June 2025, Perruzza moved that Council refuse the development application in its current form.

His motion received seven votes in favour and fourteen against and was defeated.

Council instead approved the Official Plan and zoning amendments associated with the development.

Perruzza voted against the approval motion.

This case is useful because it demonstrates an important reality about municipal politics.

A ward councillor can oppose a development, propose amendments or advocate for residents.

But the final decision may depend on the full City Council.

For accountability purposes, residents should distinguish:

**Perruzza's position:** Opposed the application in its current form.

**Council outcome:** His refusal motion lost and the planning amendments were approved.

That is more informative than either claiming he stopped the project or blaming him alone for its approval.

## Jane Street Transit Remains a Major Policy Disagreement

Transportation is another area where Perruzza has taken a clear public position.

His office is campaigning against the current proposed Jane Street bus-priority configuration and argues that Jane Street should instead maintain two general traffic lanes in each direction while accommodating reliable transit, pedestrians and cycling infrastructure.

His office argues that the current RapidTO proposal could make travel more difficult for other road users.

That is Perruzza's stated position.

However, City and TTC planning for Jane Street is driven partly by documented concerns about high transit ridership, long travel times and bus reliability.

The policy question is therefore not simply whether someone supports or opposes bus lanes.

Residents can ask:

**What alternative design is technically feasible?**

**How much would road widening cost?**

**Would additional property be required?**

**How would the alternative improve bus reliability?**

**What would happen to pedestrians, cycling facilities and general traffic?**

**How does the alternative compare with the City's current RapidTO modelling?**

Those questions would allow Perruzza's alternative to be assessed against the same practical standards as the City's proposal.

## The 119 Torbarrie Issue Is Still Not Fully Resolved

Perruzza's office is also campaigning for expanded service on the **119 Torbarrie** bus route.

That aligns with a genuine local access issue because the route currently operates as limited service rather than a full all-day, seven-day service.

The important next accountability question is:

**Has the TTC committed to an actual service expansion?**

A petition demonstrates that Perruzza is advocating for the issue.

It does not mean expanded service has been approved.

Residents should look for a TTC service decision, implementation date and published schedule before treating the request as delivered.

## Development Oversight Remains a Large Part of the Ward 7 Workload

Perruzza's office currently maintains a list of development applications across Ward 7.

Several remain under review, including projects involving:

Jane Finch Mall

2345 Finch Avenue West and 3415 Weston Road

Toryork Drive

Romfield Drive

and other locations.

The proposed first phase of the Jane Finch Mall redevelopment alone includes six new buildings and **2,730 residential units**, along with retail space, approximately 465 square metres of indoor community space and a proposed 500-square-metre daycare.

That scale of development creates important accountability questions for the ward councillor.

Residents should be able to follow:

How much affordable housing is secured?

What happens to existing businesses?

What community space is legally secured?

What childcare is actually delivered?

What transportation changes are required?

How will construction affect nearby residents?

What happens to existing tenants?

And which commitments are enforceable rather than simply proposed?

Listing development applications is useful.

The stronger test is whether residents can easily see what changed between the original proposal and final approval.

## Community Safety: Engagement Has Happened, but Residents Have Raised Continuing Concerns

Perruzza and his office have participated in community safety meetings and roundtables.

One public meeting in Shoreham Court brought together residents, City staff, Toronto Community Housing representatives, crisis-response staff and Toronto Police after community concerns about violence.

That type of direct neighbourhood engagement is useful.

But reporting from the meeting also documented residents saying that measures already introduced were not enough.

Concerns included lighting, surveillance-camera maintenance, overgrown vegetation and general property maintenance.

Because that reporting dates from 2023, it should not automatically be treated as proof that the same conditions remain unchanged in 2026.

The appropriate follow-up is:

**Which identified improvements were completed?**

**Which remain unresolved?**

**What has changed since residents raised those concerns?**

That is the type of before-and-after reporting that can show whether a community meeting produced measurable results.

**What has Council actually approved?**
