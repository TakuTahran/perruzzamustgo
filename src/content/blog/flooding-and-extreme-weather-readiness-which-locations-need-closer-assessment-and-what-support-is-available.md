*Extreme rainfall can turn a familiar street, basement or low-lying route into a serious problem very quickly.*

For residents of Ward 7, Humber River–Black Creek, flooding preparedness is particularly important because parts of the ward are connected to the Black Creek drainage system and several neighbourhoods have already been included in Toronto's Basement Flooding Protection Program studies.

But there is an important distinction.

Being inside a City flood-study area does **not** mean every property will flood.

Likewise, a location with past flooding does not automatically mean the same problem remains unchanged today.

The more useful questions are:

**Where has the City already identified a need for flood assessment or infrastructure work?**

**What locations should residents continue monitoring during intense storms?**

**And what help is available if a home or neighbourhood is affected?**

## Ward 7 Has Already Been Extensively Studied for Basement Flooding

Toronto's Basement Flooding Protection Program investigates areas where basement or surface flooding may be linked to sewer capacity, stormwater drainage and overland flow.

City records show that several flood-study areas overlap Ward 7.

They include:

**Study Area 13 – Glenfield–Jane Heights**

**Study Area 14 – Black Creek / Finch Avenue West**

**Study Area 15 – Oakdale–Beverly Heights**

**Study Area 45 – Black Creek**

**Study Area 51 – a broader study area that partly overlaps Humber River–Black Creek**

**Study Area 61 – Steeles Avenue West / Weston Road**

The City reports that the Environmental Assessment work for Ward 7's identified basement-flooding study areas has been completed. Infrastructure projects resulting from those studies are at different stages, including constructed, under construction and planned projects.

That does not mean monitoring is finished.

Flood risk can change with new development, sewer conditions, road construction, drainage changes and increasingly intense rainfall.

## Black Creek Deserves Continued Attention

Black Creek is one of the most important features to understand when discussing flooding in this part of Toronto.

Historical City planning work has documented recurring basement and surface flooding during extreme storms in areas north and south of Highway 401 along Black Creek.

The Jane Finch planning background report also noted that much of the area's older residential development was built under drainage standards developed for different storm conditions than Toronto experiences today.

This does not mean every property near Black Creek faces the same risk.

But low-lying streets, properties close to waterways and places where drainage systems have previously been studied deserve continued monitoring during severe rainfall.

## Black Creek and Finch Avenue West

The **Black Creek / Finch Avenue West** area has its own completed Basement Flooding Protection Program study.

The study designation indicates that the City has examined sewer and drainage performance in this area and identified potential infrastructure improvements.

For residents, the useful question now is not simply whether a study happened.

It is:

**Which improvements have been completed, which are still planned, and how is the drainage system performing during today's extreme storms?**

That should be checked against the City's latest Basement Flooding Protection Program project information rather than relying only on the original study date.

## Glenfield–Jane Heights

The **Glenfield–Jane Heights** area is another Ward 7 flood-study area.

Its Environmental Assessment was completed in 2014.

Because these studies generally follow sewer drainage areas rather than neighbourhood names or electoral boundaries, residents should not assume the entire neighbourhood has identical conditions.

Property-level experience can vary substantially from one street to another.

That is why reports of repeated basement flooding, overflowing catch basins or persistent road ponding should continue to be reported through 311.

Toronto specifically says reports made to 311 help the City understand how its infrastructure is performing and can help identify where future upgrades may be required.

## Oakdale–Beverly Heights

**Oakdale–Beverly Heights** is also included in Toronto's completed basement-flooding study program.

As with the other study areas, the designation should be viewed as evidence that sewer and drainage conditions have been investigated—not as evidence that every street is currently experiencing flooding.

Residents can help create a more current picture by reporting:

Repeated road ponding

Basement flooding

Overflowing catch basins

Sewer backups

Water entering underground parking

Recurring drainage problems after major storms

Those reports provide the City with current incident data rather than leaving officials to rely only on older study information.

## Steeles Avenue West and Weston Road

The City completed **Study Area 61 – Steeles Avenue West / Weston Road** in 2022.

This is one of the more recent completed studies affecting Ward 7.

The existence of a newer study is useful because infrastructure conditions and development can change significantly over time.

Residents in this area should still check current City project information to determine whether recommended improvements have moved into design, construction or later implementation stages.

## Jane–Wilson Requires Particular Flood-Aware Planning

Another important location is the **Jane and Wilson area**.

A 2025 City of Toronto planning status report states that the Jane–Wilson Visioning Study area is **entirely within a floodplain**.

Because of that condition, development in the area is regulated by both the City and the Toronto and Region Conservation Authority to reduce risks to people and property.

TRCA identifies the Jane–Wilson flood-vulnerable cluster along Black Creek as an area with dense development, low-lying roads, constrained creek crossings and locations where severe storms can produce rapid flooding.

This is stronger evidence of riverine flood exposure than simply being inside a basement-flooding study area.

Residents and planners should therefore distinguish between:

**Basement flooding risk**

and

**Riverine or overland floodplain risk.**

They can occur together, but they are not the same problem.

## Low-Lying Roads and Underpasses Need Attention During Heavy Rain

Flood readiness is not only about houses.

Roads can become dangerous very quickly during extreme rainfall.

Toronto advises residents never to drive through flooding or standing water, particularly in low-lying areas and underpasses.

Water depth can be difficult to judge, road surfaces may be damaged underneath and vehicles can become trapped.

A roadway that repeatedly ponds during intense rain deserves documentation.

Residents should record the exact location and report recurring conditions through 311 rather than relying only on photographs or social-media posts.

## Basement Flooding Should Always Be Reported

If a basement floods, Toronto advises residents to report it to **311**.

This is important even if the resident has already contacted an insurer or plumber.

The City says 311 flood reports help with several things.

They create an incident record.

They allow Toronto Water staff to investigate whether City infrastructure may be involved.

Multiple reports on the same street can help identify a sewer-system problem.

And the information contributes to decisions about future infrastructure improvements.

Under normal conditions, Toronto says a water-service technician is generally assigned to investigate within approximately four hours, although response times can be longer when large numbers of calls arrive during heavy rainfall.

## Safety Comes Before Cleanup

A flooded basement can create an electrical hazard.

Toronto advises residents **not to enter floodwater when electricity may still be present**.

If water has risen above electrical outlets or is near the electrical panel, residents should contact Toronto Hydro about disconnecting power before entering the area.

The City also advises residents to reduce unnecessary household water use if there has been a sewer backup or a backwater valve has activated.

If flooding causes property damage, residents should contact their insurance provider as soon as possible.

## Homeowners Can Receive Up to $6,650 for Flood Protection

One of the biggest changes in 2026 is the expansion of Toronto's **Basement Flooding Protection Subsidy Program**.

Beginning May 1, 2026, eligible property owners can receive up to **$6,650 per property** toward qualifying flood-protection measures.

Eligible work can include a professional home plumbing assessment, backwater valves and alarms, sump-pump systems including battery backup, and certain foundation-drain or storm-sewer disconnection work.

The program applies to eligible owners of single-family homes, duplexes, triplexes and fourplexes.

Applications and installations have specific eligibility conditions, and funding is subject to City requirements and available budget.

## A Home Plumbing Assessment Can Identify Private-Side Risk

Not every flood problem originates in the City's sewer system.

Water may enter a home because of:

Foundation cracks

Poor lot drainage

Failed weeping tiles

A malfunctioning sump pump

Connected downspouts

Blocked private sewer pipes

Or other property-specific conditions.

Toronto's expanded subsidy now includes up to **$500 toward an eligible professional home plumbing assessment**, covering up to 80 per cent of the invoiced cost.

This can help homeowners identify risks on the private side of the property before the next major storm.

## Renters Should Notify Their Landlord and Document the Damage

Renters experiencing flooding should notify the landlord or superintendent immediately and keep a written record.

Photographs, the date and time of flooding, where water entered and any damage can help create a clear timeline.

Tenants can also contact 311 when the flooding may involve City infrastructure or where municipal assistance is appropriate.

If there is an electrical hazard or immediate threat to life, emergency safety takes priority over documentation.

## Reporting Helps Identify Patterns

One flooded basement does not prove an entire neighbourhood has a systemic infrastructure problem.

But multiple documented incidents can reveal patterns.

Toronto specifically says reports of basement flooding, overflowing catch basins and sewer blockages are useful to its Basement Flooding Protection Program.

That means resident reporting is part of the City's evidence base for determining where infrastructure upgrades may be needed.

For Toronto Everyday, useful flood reporting should record the exact location, date, approximate time, rainfall conditions, type of flooding and whether the problem was reported to 311.

## Extreme Weather Is More Than Flooding

Flooding is only one part of climate and weather readiness.

Toronto identifies severe rain, power outages, heat, cold and winter storms among the emergencies residents should prepare for.

The City advises households to have an emergency plan and supplies available because during a major emergency it may take as long as **72 hours** for emergency services to reach everyone who needs assistance.

A household emergency kit can be especially important when flooding is accompanied by a power outage or transportation disruption.

## Extreme Heat Also Requires Neighbourhood Preparedness

Toronto's 2026 Heat Relief Strategy operates during the warm season and focuses particularly on people at increased risk from heat.

The City's Heat Relief Network includes **more than 500 cooling spaces** such as libraries, community centres, civic buildings, pools and partner facilities.

These spaces are available throughout the summer rather than only when a formal Heat Warning is issued.

This matters for Ward 7 residents living in apartments without adequate cooling, seniors, people with health vulnerabilities and households with young children.

Toronto has also operated targeted cooling-device assistance for eligible vulnerable households during the 2026 heat season.

## What Locations Deserve Closer Assessment?

Based on existing City and TRCA records, closer monitoring should focus on **conditions**, not simply neighbourhood labels.

Particular attention is justified where several factors overlap:

A property or road lies within or near a mapped floodplain.

The location has a documented history of basement or surface flooding.

Residents repeatedly report flooding through 311.

Roads or pedestrian routes experience recurring ponding.

A neighbourhood is within a Basement Flooding Protection Program study area where recommended projects remain under construction or planned.

New development may increase pressure on drainage or sewer infrastructure.

And residents report that current conditions differ from what older studies recorded.

This approach avoids labelling an entire neighbourhood as flood-prone when the evidence may apply only to specific drainage areas or properties.

## What Should Ward 7 Residents Watch Next?

The most important follow-up is implementation.

Toronto has already completed flood studies covering Ward 7.

Residents can now ask:

**Which recommended sewer upgrades have been completed?**

**Which projects are under construction?**

**Which remain planned?**

**Where are repeated 311 flood reports still occurring?**

**Have recent storms revealed new locations requiring investigation?**

**Are new developments being assessed against current flood and stormwater conditions?**

**Are residents receiving clear information about available protection programs?**

Those questions provide a stronger picture of neighbourhood readiness than simply asking whether Toronto has conducted a study.

## Preparation Before the Next Storm Matters

Flood protection works best before water starts entering a home.

Residents can review their insurance coverage, understand whether their property is located near a floodplain, keep drains and catch basins clear when safe to do so, inspect property drainage and consider whether eligible flood-protection equipment is appropriate for their home.

During extreme rain, residents should follow official weather and flood alerts, avoid low-lying flooded roads and stay away from creeks and rapidly moving water.

After flooding, residents should prioritize safety, report the incident to 311 and document the condition.
